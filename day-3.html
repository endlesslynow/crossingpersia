Day 3 · 19 November, 500 BCE

Eleusis to Oinoe

◆ ◆ Map
دانلود صدا

The Thriasian plain, the flat farmland behind Eleusis, was full of ploughing today. Pairs of oxen leaned into their yokes, men walked behind them holding the ploughs steady, and boys followed with bags of seed at their hips, throwing it out in wide handfuls. The turned earth was dark and wet and smelled sweet. At the edge of one field a farmer with no oxen was breaking the ground with a hoe, alone.

Cranes came over in the afternoon, so high that I heard them before I found them, a long wavering line crying across the sky. Every farm boy in Attica knows the verse of Hesiod, the old farmer-poet of Boeotia, that the crane crying from the clouds means it's time to plough. Hesiod also says the same cry grieves the heart of the man who has no oxen. I looked back at the man with the hoe, still bent over his field.

After the plain I climbed for the rest of the afternoon, until I came out into the high plain of Oinoe, a small valley of its own with fields, scattered farms and smoke rising from the village. Glaukos, a farmer of Oinoe, let me sleep in his house for a few small coins, and the donkey got a corner of his yard and an armful of straw.

Photo: A drinking cup made in Athens about 560 to 550 BCE, showing a boy sowing seed and a man ploughing with oxen. It was found at Kameiros on Rhodes and is in the British Museum, London (1906,1215.1). Photographed in 2018. Photo Credit: Photo by ArchaiOptix, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

Based on evidence: Travellers on the road from Eleusis to Thebes crossed the Thriasian plain for about 5 km, then climbed into the plain of Oinoe, 310 metres above the sea. The village of Oinoe existed in the last quarter of the 500s BCE. Hesiod, a Boeotian farmer-poet of about 700 BCE, wrote that the crane crying from the clouds each year was the signal for ploughing, and that its cry "vexes the heart of the man who has no oxen".
